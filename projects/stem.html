STEM: STructural EMbedding for Graph Representation of 3D Designs

Xinghao Peng, Runsang Liu, Hui Yang

Complex Systems Monitoring, Modeling and Control Laboratory · The Pennsylvania State University

Paper Slides · soon Code · soon BibTeX

Overview

A self-organizing graph represents a 3D design as a sparse matrix, which opens complex geometry to graph theory. Few studies, however, have distilled intrinsic shape characteristics from these matrices to match similar geometric structures.

STEM is a feature-learning framework that encodes each sparse matrix into a STructural EMbedding for matching and retrieving 3D designs across categories such as vehicles, prosthetics and airplanes. The aim is a searchable repository of 3D designs that carries accumulated design knowledge into manufacturing analytics, for example reusing validated support structures and toolpath settings in additive manufacturing.

How it works

  1. Self-organizing graph. Each design is voxelized, nearby voxels are connected into a sparse adjacency matrix, and the graph settles under a spring–electrical model until its energy reaches a steady state.
  2. Graph signature. Permutation-invariant global patterns (degree and clustering histograms, Laplacian and adjacency spectra, shortest paths, centrality moments and adjacency-matrix traces) plus an 11-dimensional feature vector for every node.
  3. Dual-channel encoder. A graph attention (GAT) branch learns local patterns from the node features, and an MLP branch fuses them with the global signature into a 128-dimensional STEM.
  4. Hierarchical loss. Three levels pull variants of the same design together, keep subcategories compact and push main categories apart, so the embedding is robust to rotation and node relabeling.

Framework

3D designs become self-organizing graphs, a dual-channel encoder trained with a hierarchical loss maps them to STEMs, and the embeddings support matching and clustering that hold up under node, edge and permutation variation.

Results

Two datasets from the Princeton Shape Benchmark: 210 designs in 14 main categories and 21 subcategories, and 131 designs in 8 main categories and 12 subcategories.

  • Robust to rotation and relabeling. With 50 random rotation and permutation variants per design, 95.8% and 93.9% of unseen variants land within their design's acceptance radius on the two datasets, compared with 92.1% and 91.4% without the GAT branch.
  • Label matching. 92.9% main-category and 90.2% subcategory accuracy on Dataset 1, against roughly 70–83% for random forest, softmax, SVM and XGBoost trained directly on the raw global signature vectors.
  • Retrieval. 87.5% subcategory mean average precision on Dataset 1, compared with 82.3% for the MLP-only ablation.
